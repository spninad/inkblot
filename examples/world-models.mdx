<Section number="1" title="Introduction" id="intro">

<Subsection number="1.1" title="The problem" id="intro-prob">

Many control problems of practical interest share an inconvenient property: the real system is costly to experiment with. A robot that falls may break; a wind tunnel or a fluid simulation may take hours per trial; a chemical process cannot be run thousands of times to learn by trial and error. Reinforcement learning methods that learn purely from interaction typically require millions of trials, which is not feasible in such settings.

A widely studied alternative is to learn a *model* of the system from a modest amount of data and then to plan inside that model. The agent imagines the outcomes of many possible action sequences, selects the most promising, executes only the first part of it in the real system, observes what actually happened, and repeats.

The approach has three well-known points of fragility:

- **The model can become wrong.** The physical conditions at deployment may differ from those seen during training; a robot may carry a heavier load, or a surface may be more slippery.
- **The model can be uncertain.** After only a few observations, several different explanations of the system's behaviour may fit the data equally well.
- **Planning is expensive.** Imagining thousands of futures at every step costs substantial computation.

<Question>
How should an intelligent agent learn and make decisions when interaction with the real world is expensive, its current model is incomplete, and data is limited, so that it cannot simply collect millions of new examples?
</Question>

</Subsection>

<Section number="2" title="Background and Architecture" id="background">

<Subsection number="2.1" title="Model-predictive control loop" id="bg-mpc">

In model-predictive control (MPC), the agent does not learn a fixed mapping from situations to actions. Instead, at every decision point it searches for a good sequence of future actions by simulating candidates inside its world model.

<Aside title="Note on Latent Space Prediction">
When the environment is observed through images, predicting every pixel of the next frame is both difficult and largely unnecessary. Modern world models therefore work in a <em>latent space</em>: an encoder network compresses each observation into a vector of a few hundred numbers.
</Aside>

</Subsection>

<Subsection number="2.2" title="Mathematical Objective" id="bg-math">

The energy score loss used for multi-hypothesis training balances candidate accuracy with hypothesis diversity:

<Equation math="\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}(y, \hat{Y}) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \|y - \hat{y}_k\|_2 - \frac{1}{2K^2}\sum_{j=1}^K\sum_{k=1}^K \|\hat{y}_j - \hat{y}_k\|_2" />

Here $y$ denotes the ground-truth transition, and $\hat{Y} = \{\hat{y}_1, \dots, \hat{y}_K\}$ represents the ensemble of $K$ candidate predictions.

</Subsection>

<Collapse summary="View detailed glossary of terms">

<Glossary>
  <GlossaryItem term="World Model">
    A learned function that, given the current state of an environment and an action, predicts the next state.
  </GlossaryItem>
  <GlossaryItem term="Latent State">
    A compressed vector representation of high-dimensional observations produced by an encoder network.
  </GlossaryItem>
  <GlossaryItem term="Model-Predictive Control">
    An algorithm that optimizes future action trajectories by rolling them out within an internal model.
  </GlossaryItem>
  <GlossaryItem term="Test-Time Adaptation">
    Updating world model weights online using state observations gathered during real deployment.
  </GlossaryItem>
</Glossary>
